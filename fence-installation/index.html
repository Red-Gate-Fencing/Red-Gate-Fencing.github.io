A successful fence installation in Camp Hill, Harrisburg, or elsewhere in Cumberland County starts well before the first post hole. The homeowners who are happiest with a new fence usually made a few decisions early: what the fence has to do, which material fits that job, and what the property and local rules will allow. This guide walks through that planning sequence so a site visit and estimate are more useful.

Decide what the fence needs to do

Write a short purpose statement before you shop styles. A backyard in Camp Hill or Mechanicsburg may need privacy from a neighboring deck or a busy street. A family with dogs may care more about a secure, durable enclosure than about full visual screening. A front-yard project may be about curb appeal and a clean boundary rather than seclusion. A commercial lot may need a practical chain link perimeter. A farm or large rural property may need livestock containment and equipment access.

Those purposes can be combined, but they should be ranked. A tall privacy fence and an open aluminum fence both count as fence installation, yet they solve different problems. Red Gate Fencing installs privacy, wood, vinyl, aluminum, chain link, and farm fencing for homes, businesses, and agricultural properties, which means the first conversation can be about the job the fence has to do rather than a single product.

Choosing a material that fits the property

Once the purpose is clear, match material to use, look, and maintenance:

  • Wood remains a classic choice for warmth, privacy, and a natural look. It can be built as a picket fence or a taller privacy fence. Plan on more upkeep over time than vinyl or aluminum, especially through humid summers and wet winters.
  • Vinyl is often selected for privacy or semi-privacy when a homeowner wants less long-term maintenance and a material that resists warping.
  • Aluminum offers a clean, rust-resistant look with minimal upkeep and is frequently used for front yards, pools, and decorative borders. It does not typically provide the same visual screening as a privacy fence.
  • Chain link is a practical, low-maintenance option for yards, play areas, pet containment, and commercial security.
  • Farm fencing should be chosen with animal type, terrain, and acreage in mind, not by applying a residential backyard detail to a pasture.

There is no single “best” fence for the Harrisburg area. There is a best fit for your lot, your goals, and the amount of maintenance you want to take on. A site visit is the right time to test those assumptions against grade, drainage, and neighboring properties. Preparing the site and the paperwork

Installation goes more smoothly when the line is clear and the rules are understood. Walk the proposed route and move grills, play equipment, planters, and stored materials out of the way. Tell the installer about irrigation lines, a buried pet containment wire, landscaping you want to keep, or a tight access path along a garage.

On the paperwork side, confirm property lines and ask about permits and association guidelines for your address. Height limits, style rules, and setback requirements vary by municipality and by HOA. Red Gate Fencing notes that its licensed and insured crew follows local permitting and property line requirements. You still need to know where your line is and whether a survey, permit, or architectural review applies. If utilities have not been located, ask how that will be handled before digging begins. Calling 811 to request utility locates is a standard precaution on any project that involves holes in the ground.

What happens during installation

After an estimate is accepted, a typical installation sequence includes a final confirmation of the layout, post setting, assembly of panels or fabric, hanging and adjusting gates, and cleanup. The exact order depends on the material and the site. Ask for a simple sequence and a sense of how long the crew expects to be on site, including any time the yard will be less usable. Red Gate Fencing lists fast turnaround among the reasons homeowners choose the company, and also says it does not cut corners on workmanship or safety. If you have a deadline — a dog arriving, a pool season, or a property closing — say so early, and ask what is realistic for your project rather than assuming a standard number of days.

Stay reachable during the work. Questions about an unmarked line, an unexpected obstacle, or a gate location are easier to settle the same day. If you will not be home, leave a contact number and any access instructions.

Caring for the fence after installation

Use the final walkthrough to operate every gate and look at the full line. Ask what routine care fits the material you chose. Wood fences generally need periodic cleaning and a finish or stain to help them weather well. Vinyl and aluminum are lower maintenance but still benefit from occasional cleaning. Chain link should be checked for tension, damaged mesh, and gate hardware. Farm fencing should be inspected along the runs that take the most pressure from animals or equipment.

Keep the company’s contact information. Red Gate Fencing says it aims to be there for customers after the project is complete. If something needs attention, it is better to call the installer who knows the job than to guess at a fix.

Common questions

How should I prepare my yard for fence installation?

Clear personal items along the proposed line, unlock gates or side yards the crew will need, and point out sprinklers, buried pet fence, or landscaping you want protected. Ask the installer what else they need from you before the first work day.

Do I need a permit for a new fence?

It depends on the municipality, the height and location of the fence, and any association rules. Red Gate Fencing says its crew follows local permitting and property line requirements, but you should still ask what is needed for your address and confirm the rules that apply to your lot.

What fence material is best for privacy?

Wood and vinyl privacy fences are the most common choices for blocking sightlines. Wood offers a natural look; vinyl typically needs less long-term maintenance and resists warping. The better choice depends on budget, style, and how much upkeep you want to handle.
