Start with the property, not a catalog photo

Before you call, write down what you want the fence to do. What a site visit should cover

Red Gate Fencing, a family-owned company based at 118 Yellow Breeches Drive in Camp Hill, says every job starts with a site visit so the estimate reflects the work on that specific site. That visit is your chance to walk the line with the contractor and ask them to think out loud.

A thorough visit usually covers the proposed route, fence height and style, gate locations, how posts will be set, and whether an existing fence must be removed. It should also include a look at access for materials and equipment, any obvious drainage or soil concerns, and a conversation about property lines. The company’s site notes that its licensed and insured crew follows local permitting and property line requirements. You should still confirm where your line sits, whether a survey is needed, and whether a municipality or homeowners association has rules about height, style, or setback.

If you have already marked corners, pulled a plot plan, or called 811 to locate utilities, bring that information. If you have not, ask the contractor how they handle utility locates and what they need from you before installation day.

How to compare estimates fairly

A free, no-obligation estimate is only useful if you can tell what it includes. Ask for the material, height, and approximate length in writing, along with the number and width of gates, the post-setting method, old-fence removal, haul-away, and site cleanup. Ask what could change the scope once work begins — hidden concrete, rock, a disputed line, or a change in gate hardware are common examples.

Then compare contractors with the same questions. One bid may look lower because it leaves demolition, downhill grading, or a second gate out of the price. Another may specify a sturdier post schedule or a different grade of material. Red Gate Fencing’s owners, Stephen and Erica Snyder, have said they built the company around clear communication and straightforward pricing after seeing how often homeowners were left with confusing estimates. Use that standard when you read any proposal, including theirs: you should be able to explain the work back in plain language.

Questions worth asking any fence contractor

  • Who will be on site, and is the work handled by the company’s own crew?
  • Are you licensed and insured for this project, and can you provide proof?
  • How do you confirm property lines and local permit requirements?
  • How will posts be set for this soil and for freeze-thaw weather in Central Pennsylvania?
  • What is the expected sequence and how will you communicate changes?
  • What should I do to prepare the yard, and what cleanup is included?
  • How are gates, corners, and changes in grade handled on a lot like mine?

Listen for answers that refer to your property rather than a generic promise that every fence turns out the same. A contractor who has already looked at the lot should be able to talk about your slope, your neighbors, and your preferred material in specific terms.

Common questions

What should I have ready before I call a fence contractor?

Be ready to describe the property, the length or area you want fenced, the main purpose of the fence, and any known constraints such as a slope, an existing fence, an easement, or an association rule. Photos of the yard and a rough idea of preferred materials help, but they do not replace a site visit.

Why does a fence contractor need to visit the property?

A site visit lets the contractor see grade, soil, access, corners, gates, and existing structures that photos can miss. Red Gate Fencing starts jobs with a site visit so the estimate can reflect the work on that specific property.

How do I compare fence contractor estimates?

Compare the same scope: material and height, post setting, gates, removal of an old fence, cleanup, and what could change the work once it starts. A lower number is not a better bid if it leaves out work the other estimate includes.
